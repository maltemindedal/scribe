The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as he had every morning for thirty years. He climbed the
spiral stairs, counting the steps out of habit, and checked the lamp, the lens and the clockwork
that turned them. Outside, the sea was grey and calm, and a thin line of light was growing in the
east. He wrote the time, the weather and the state of the lamp in the log book, then made tea on
the small iron stove and sat by the window to watch the fishing boats leave the harbour.

Most days nothing happened. A gull would land on the railing, a ship would pass far out on the
horizon, and the wind would change direction in the afternoon. But the keeper liked the quiet. He
had learned the names of the birds and the shapes of the clouds, and he could tell from the colour
of the water whether a storm was coming. When storms did come, he stayed awake all night, keeping
the lamp bright and the lens clean, because somewhere in the dark a sailor might be looking for
the light.

One winter a young woman arrived on the supply boat. She had been sent by the harbour office to
learn the work, because the keeper was getting old and someone would have to take his place. At
first she found the routine dull. She asked why he wrote the same words in the log every day, and
why he polished brass that was already shining. He told her that a lighthouse is a promise, and a
promise is only worth something if it is kept every single time, not just when it is convenient.

Over the months she began to understand. She learned to read the sea, to trim the wick, to wind
the clockwork and to sleep in short pieces during a storm. In spring, when the keeper finally
left on the supply boat, she stood on the gallery and watched until the boat was a dot on the
water. Then she went inside, climbed the stairs, counted the steps, and wrote the time, the
weather and the state of the lamp in the log book.

Years later, children in the village asked her whether she was ever lonely out there. She said
that she had the birds, the clouds, the ships and the light, and that every night someone she
would never meet was trusting her to be awake. That, she said, was enough company for anyone.
